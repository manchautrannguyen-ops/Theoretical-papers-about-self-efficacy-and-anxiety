Table summarizing six sources of language anxiety, their details, and reduction strategies from Young (1991)

Source of anxiety Details How to reduce it
Personal & interpersonal Low self-esteem and competitiveness are the two main drivers. Students compare themselves to peers or an idealized self-image (Bailey). Krashen links low self-esteem directly to anxiety. Price found most students believed their skills were weaker than classmates'. Related constructs include communication apprehension, social anxiety, stage fright, and "existential anxiety" (Rardin) — the fear that learning another language threatens one's identity. Krashen's "club membership" and Terrell's "target language group identification" also play a role.
  • Have students verbalize fears aloud; write them on the board so they see they are not alone (Foss & Reitzel)
  • Use anxiety graphs to help students identify peak anxiety moments in interactions
  • Encourage reflective journal writing to surface feelings of inadequacy
  • Facilitate role-play activities like "Agony Column" (Crookall & Oxford) to explore anxieties in a structured way
  • Offer supplemental support groups, tutoring, language clubs, and relaxation exercises
  • Teach productive self-talk strategies to counter anxious inner monologue
Learner beliefs Unrealistic beliefs cause anxiety when they clash with reality. Common problematic beliefs include: pronunciation is the most important skill; two years is enough to become fluent; some people simply cannot learn languages; language learning is primarily about translating from English. When beginners discover they do not sound native-like, frustration and stress follow.
  • Discuss realistic goals and the value of partial fluency (Horwitz)
  • Directly challenge erroneous beliefs with new information — many students have never had these beliefs questioned
  • Return to these discussions periodically throughout the course, not just at the start
Instructor beliefs Instructors who view their role as authority figure or "drill sergeant" raise anxiety. In Brandl's study, most instructors felt a little intimidation was a necessary motivator, and objected to overly friendly student–teacher relationships. Constant error correction, belief that pair work causes loss of control, and an instructor-dominated classroom all contribute.
  • Sensitize instructors to their role as facilitators rather than authority figures
  • Encourage instructors to examine their own beliefs through videotaping lessons or reciprocal class visits
  • Complete the Beliefs about Language Learning Inventory (BALLI)
  • Participate in language teaching workshops and conferences to stay current
Instructor–learner interactions Harsh error correction is a frequently cited anxiety trigger. Students report anxiety about responding incorrectly in front of peers, about looking or sounding "dumb," and about how mistakes are perceived. However, learners also report that some correction feels necessary — so the issue is not correction itself, but the manner, frequency, and tone of correction.
  • Adopt an attitude that mistakes are a normal, universal part of language learning
  • Use modeling — repeat the correct form naturally rather than spotlighting the student's error
  • Give positive reinforcement and help students set realistic expectations (Price)
  • Be friendly, patient, and encourage students to speak out (Young)
  • Reward successful communication, not only grammatical accuracy (Brandl's grading approach)
  • Use "Mistakes Panel" activity to help students see errors as non-taboo (Crookall & Oxford)
Classroom procedures Activities requiring public speaking are the most anxiety-provoking: oral presentations, skits in front of the class, oral quizzes, and being called on randomly. Over 68% of Young's subjects felt more comfortable not having to speak in front of the class. Alphabetical seating and cold-calling are also implicated.
  • Use small-group and pair work to reduce public-speaking pressure
  • Personalize instruction — link vocabulary and grammar to students' own lives
  • Make content so compelling that students forget they are speaking another language (Krashen)
  • Use language games focused on problem-solving rather than competitive formats
  • Precede high-anxiety activities (e.g., role plays) with rapport-building tasks
  • Allow oral interpretation practice in small groups before any public performance
  • Avoid random cold-calling, alphabetical seating, and mandatory oral readings (Daly)
Language testing Anxiety rises when test formats differ from what was taught, when question types are unfamiliar, or when tests feel ambiguous and highly evaluative. Madsen et al. found that some test item types produce significantly more anxiety than others. A communicative teaching approach paired with grammar-only tests is a particular mismatch.
  • Test what is taught in the way it was taught — align test format with instruction
  • Expose students to test item types in class before the test (pre-test practice)
  • Clearly communicate performance expectations with worked examples
  • Reduce ambiguity in test tasks — familiarity with the format lowers anxiety
