Cognitive Locus of Operation

Bandura argues that earlier behavioral theories relied on peripheral mechanisms — behavior was shaped automatically by stimuli and consequences, without conscious . Evidence from multiple lines of research shifted this view. The section establishes that cognition plays a central role in how behavior is , retained, and regulated. Key points include: behavior is coded in symbolic memory and learned through modeling; learning from consequences is itself a cognitive process (people discern patterns, not just react); reinforcement works through expectations it creates, not through automatic strengthening; and motivation is partly driven by cognitive representations of future outcomes and self-evaluative standards.

Efficacy Expectations as a Mechanism of Operation

Bandura introduces the core distinction between two types of expectations: outcome expectations (belief that a behavior will produce certain outcomes) and efficacy expectations (conviction that one can actually execute the required behavior). The two are different — a person can believe an action would work while doubting their ability to perform it. Efficacy expectations influence whether behavior is initiated, how much effort is applied, and how long effort is sustained in the face of obstacles. Stronger perceived self-efficacy leads to more active, coping. However, self-efficacy is not the sole determinant — requisite skills and incentives must also be present.

Dimensions of Efficacy Expectations

Efficacy expectations are not a single, static factor. They vary along three important dimensions: magnitude (how difficult the tasks a person believes they can handle), (whether mastery expectations extend beyond specific treatment situations to broader contexts), and strength (how firmly expectations are held — weak expectations are easily undermined by setbacks, while strong ones persist despite failure). Bandura criticizes prior research for using global, outcome-focused measures that conflate hope, wishful thinking, and faith in the therapist with genuine self-efficacy. Adequate research must all three dimensions at multiple points throughout the change process.

Sources of Efficacy Expectations

Four main sources inform people's sense of efficacy:

Performance accomplishments

The most powerful source, as it is based on direct personal . Successes raise efficacy; early failures lower it. of efficacy to similar situations occurs after mastery is established. Performance-based treatments (e.g., participant modeling) consistently outperform symbolic ones.

Vicarious experience

Observing others succeed raises the observer's sense of capability through social . This is a weaker, less reliable source than direct experience. Its effectiveness is enhanced by coping (who overcome struggle), similar models, clear outcomes, and diversified modeling.

Verbal persuasion

Widely used but limited in power. alone cannot create a durable sense of efficacy, especially in the face of a long history of failure. It works best in conjunction with performance aids and is unlikely to produce change if not supported by actual successes.

Physiological/emotional arousal

People use their internal states as a cue to judge their capability. High signals vulnerability; reduced arousal signals greater competence. Treatments that extinguish anxiety (e.g., desensitization) can raise efficacy, though arousal is the least source. False or misattributed arousal feedback has little lasting effect on behavior.

Cognitive Processing of Efficacy Information

The impact of information on self-efficacy depends on how it is cognitively . For instance, successes might be discounted if they occur under highly safeguarded conditions or are to external aids rather than personal skill. Conversely, successes achieved with minimal effort foster a stronger sense of ability than those requiring high effort. Self-directed mastery is essential to authenticate personal efficacy and ensure that gains are not insulated from real-life application.

Differing Perspectives on Self-efficacy

Bandura compares his theory to two related constructs. Against effectance motivation (White, 1959) — an intrinsic drive for competence — Bandura argues his social learning approach is more : self-efficacy is measured independently of performance, varies by situation, and arises from diverse identifiable sources (not just novel stimulation). Against locus of control (Rotter, 1966) — beliefs about whether outcomes are controlled internally or externally — Bandura distinguishes that locus of control is about , not personal capability. A person can believe outcomes are personally determined while still doubting their own ability, which social learning theory treats as a separate and consequential construct. He also contrasts his theory with learned helplessness (Seligman), noting that giving up can stem from either low efficacy expectations or low outcome expectations, and these have different remedies.

Microanalysis of Self-efficacy and Behavioral Change

Bandura reports an experiment with severe snake phobics assigned to participant modeling, modeling only, or no treatment. The study used a method: measuring level, strength, and generality of efficacy expectations at multiple points, task by task. Key findings: participant modeling produced the highest and most efficacy expectations, followed by modeling alone, then controls. Crucially, self-efficacy was an accurate predictor of subsequent performance at the individual task level — ~89% congruence for enactive treatment and ~86% for vicarious — regardless of how the efficacy was induced. Stronger efficacy expectations were consistently associated with higher probability of successful task completion. Efficacy expectations predicted generalized behavior toward novel, dissimilar threats better than past performance did.

Comparison of Self-efficacy and Dual-Process Theory

This section pits self-efficacy theory against dual-process (anxiety-drive) theory, which underlies standard desensitization. Dual-process theory holds that anxiety drives avoidance behavior, so eliminating anxiety should eliminate avoidance. Bandura critiques this: evidence shows autonomic arousal is not causally necessary for avoidance learning or maintenance; avoidance can outlast anxiety ; and anxiety reduction alone produces variable behavior change. Social learning theory instead treats anxiety and defensive behavior as co-effects of perceived , not causally linked. In an experiment where snake phobics were fully desensitized (anxiety extinguished to imagined threats), subjects emerged with widely differing efficacy expectations — and behavior corresponded closely to those efficacy levels (r = .74), not to the degree of anxiety extinction. Self-efficacy microanalysis showed 85% congruence between expectations and performance.

Microanalysis of Self-efficacy and Performance During the Process of Change

A further study tracked the evolution of efficacy expectations and behavior during treatment (not just at its end), using participant modeling administered in sequential blocks of increasing difficulty. Key finding: subjects who achieved the same partial mastery during treatment varied considerably in what they could subsequently do — past performance alone was a poor . This confirms that efficacy expectations, not behavioral history, are the operative mechanism of change.
